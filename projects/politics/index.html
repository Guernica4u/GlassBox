How Veyland works

Veyland is a made-up country of 600 citizens. Nothing anyone does is scripted. Everybody starts knowing nothing and learns from their own experience.

Citizens feel six needs (money, health, safety, freedom, fairness, dignity at work). Each year they compare how their life changed with how the laws changed, and slowly learn which laws help or hurt them. A factory worker, a farmer and a factory owner learn different things. They vote for whoever promises the laws they believe in, and trust parties that governed while life got better. They also chat with neighbours and pick up their opinions.

Each month they choose to stay quiet, protest, strike or rise up, and learn from what happened next: did life improve, did they get arrested? A crowd nearby, anger, and believing in a better system push them to act. Strong police hold them back. If the people (and police) who want a different system outweigh those defending it, there's a revolution or coup, and the country becomes whatever most rebels wanted: communism, syndicalism, mutualism, distributism, fascism, technocracy, a free-market minarchy, a military junta, or liberal democracy.

Parties have a small brain (a neural network) that turns the state of the country into promises. Before each election, each party tests six versions of its brain in an opinion poll (a primary). The winner leads, and the next six are variations of it, plus ideas copied from rivals. That's evolution: promises that win votes survive. Dictators evolve the same way, but they test plans on their own supporters, especially the police. Experts (technocracy) test plans with the economic model.

Lobbies (business, unions, smallholders) learn whether saving, donating to a party or bribing the government helps their members. Governments learn whether taking bribes pays, but a free press finds some of them out.

Changing the system peacefully is possible but very hard. Parties can campaign for a new system. If parties holding two-thirds of parliament back the same one in two elections running, there is a referendum, and 60% must say yes. Workers' councils and assemblies need two-thirds wanting the same system for two years. A dictatorship hands over power only if most of its own police and most people want the same system and it is very unpopular.

War: next door is Ostria, a made-up neighbour. It may attack when Veyland looks weak or torn apart. Parties and rulers can also choose war. Citizens learn whether war helps or hurts them, the same way they learn about laws. Soldiers die, the economy suffers, and a defeat can bring a government down.

The press can be bought. Lobbies may buy newspapers that spin the news their owners' way and bury their owners' scandals. Where speech is not free, the state runs the media and never exposes the rulers. Regional parties form when a region feels nobody speaks for it; they stand only at home. Emigration: people leave when they believe life abroad is better, judged from letters home by those who left. Dictatorships close the borders. Immigrants arrive when Veyland looks good, knowing nothing.

Real-life politics: up to 8 parties at once, each with a named leader whose charisma wins or loses votes. Leaders resign after bad results, have scandals, grow old and die; when a dictator dies there is a succession struggle. Big parties can split when their own voters feel abandoned, small similar ones merge, and coalitions can collapse between elections.

Events happen the way they do in real life, more or less likely depending on the laws: mine disasters and factory fires (likelier with weak safety rules), floods, earthquakes and epidemics (good public services soften them), market crashes (likelier after booms with light regulation), inventions (likelier with good schools and free speech), price shocks, border incidents, trade deals, sanctions on dictatorships, refugee waves, assassination attempts and sporting triumphs. Regions hit by a disaster are circled on the map.

Systems: besides liberal democracy there are communism, syndicalism, mutualism, distributism, fascism, technocracy, free-market minarchy, military junta, national syndicalism, absolute monarchy, constitutional monarchy, monarcho-distributism, anarcho-communism and theocracy (rule by the Lantern Church, Veyland's made-up old faith). Parties drift between about 30 sects, from eco-socialists and market socialists to religious democrats, agrarians and populists of left and right.

Parties come and go: young voters drift away from their grandparents' parties, and old declining parties may rebrand. Democracies can backslide: a strongman or anti-democratic party with a majority slowly packs the courts and leans on the press; mass protests slow it and losing power reverses it, but if it goes all the way, elections are suspended.

Communities: the Lantern Church, workers' clubs, farmers' co-ops, student societies, business clubs, veterans and newcomers, plus street movements (Anti-fascist Action, patriotic and workers' militias, green activists). People join for belonging and ideals and leave when it costs too much. Members talk mostly with each other; movements bring people onto the streets, militias are armed, and rival movements clash.
